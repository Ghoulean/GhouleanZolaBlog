I'm writing this guide because I sometimes feel like I'm playing alongside my friends instead of playing with my friends when I play multiplayer games with them. While I'll be using League of Legends as illustrating examples, I believe these concepts extend to all team-based video games.

# General Etiquette

Make sure the things you say and the way you say it don't piss people off and make them not want to listen to you. It doesn't matter if you hard carry every game; if you're a jerk people will eventually stop wanting to play with you. The easiest way to do this is to consistently practice sportsmanlike behavior.

This is the bare minimum for sportsmanlike communication:

1. Don't grief your team
2. Don't attack, blame, or criticize others
3. Don't whine or complain about your circumstances
4. If you have nothing nonnegative to say, say nothing

Breaking any of these rules accomplishes nothing but bring down your team's morale and your own morale.

Note that points 2, 3, and 4 involve doing nothing instead of doing something. Doing nothing is very easy; a jagged rock by the riverside is able to accomplish this.

Once you've mastered General Etiquette 101, it's time to move onto General Etiquette 102:

1. Sincerely compliment others when you think they do something good.
- Note that the threshold for "something good" can be as simple as getting a single kill; the biggest qualifier is "sincere". This is a skill that you need to practice; if you're not used to giving or receiving compliments, the first several attempts will feel forced and awkward.
- Backhanded compliments aren't sincere.
- You don't need to be complex; a simple "nice" suffices
- You don't need to do this particularly often; once or twice per game is enough
2. Make people feel like the carry
- This is similar to point 1 and overlaps somewhat. Make people feel like their good actions are contributing to winning the game
- It doesn't matter if their scoreline is 10/1 or 1/10. They're helping
- You don't need to be complicated. Again, a simple "good job" or "nice <what they did>" suffices

I'd like to emphasize again that these actions are incredibly easy. If you somehow manage to not only break these rules, but break them consistently, you will get thrown out with no chance of redemption.

Warning: when you offend someone, even if only slightly, you should not expect pushback. They are not obligated to criticize you, and thus the majority of socially well-adjusted people will not criticize you (see general etiquette point 2). They will simply smile politely, then avoid you.

# Importance and Urgency

Every piece of information in your brain falls somewhere on [the scale of "importance" and "urgency"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_management#Eisenhower_method). Let's go through a few examples.

1. The enemy jungler is en route to gank your teammate. You notice, but your teammate doesn't. Both important and urgent
2. You think we should prepare for a dragon fight soon because it will respawn in a minute. Important, but not urgent
3. You're the jungler, and the enemy is splitpushing botlane. Suppose you cannot address the splitpusher (e.g. you're the jungler and must be present for Baron), but someone else on your team should. Not important (for you), but urgent
4. Darius is overpowered, and your opponent is a scrub for playing him. Neither important nor urgent (even if it's true)

Generally speaking, you should communicate information that is important, urgent, or both, and you should not communicate information that is neither.

# Loudness

Some modes of communication are a lot more noticable than others:

1. If you're in a voice chat (VC), talking to your teammates is louder than typing in chat.
2. Some games such as League of Legends and Apex Legends lets you "ping" what you want to do, which is roughly as loud as typing in chat. If I had to choose one or the other, I would say that pinging is slightly louder than typing
3. Typing in chat is the next-loudest action. However, it's very easy to miss callouts in chat, especially if your teammate is in the middle of a fight or some other micro-intensive play
4. Body language is the quietest action. By body language, I'm referring to how you control your character in-game, such as your micro movements, your positioning, and the direction you're facing. With a few exceptions (such as ADC/support), most players will spend all of their brainpower paying attention to their opponent's body language. Thus, you want to leak as little information to your opponent as possible while not misleading your teammates

# Time and Place

Combining the two above, good communication becomes simple:

1. In-game, communicate things that are urgent
2. In-game, communicate things that are important, but also urgent enough that it cannot be saved for post game
3. Use the appropriate loudness when communicating in-game
4. 

- in game: say things that are urgent
- using the appropriate loudness
- not urgent? ideally say it pre-game. or save it for postgame
